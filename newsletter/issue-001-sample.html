Perspective
 

The secondary price is not a price

Somebody will tell you a bottle “goes for” four hundred dollars. They are quoting a secondary sale, and a secondary sale measures exactly one thing: what the most impatient person in the room paid on the day they ran out of patience.

That is not what a price is. A price is where supply and demand meet repeatedly, across many buyers, over time. Secondary bourbon numbers are the opposite — thin volume, self-selected buyers, no obligation to disclose the sales that didn’t happen. Every listing that sat unsold for a month vanishes from the record. You only ever see the top of the distribution and mistake it for the middle.

This matters because the secondary number leaks backward into the shelf. A store owner reads the same forum you do, sees four hundred, and prices at two-fifty feeling generous. Now a bottle that cost sixty dollars to make and forty to distribute carries a price set by a stranger’s bad Tuesday. The hype becomes the input to the cost.

The way out is boring and it works: decide what a bottle is worth to you before you learn what anyone paid. Proof, age, mashbill, and how it actually drinks next to the three bottles already open on your counter. That number barely moves year over year. The secondary number moves every time a video posts.

This is the whole reason What We’d Pay exists. It is not a prediction of what you’ll find, and it is emphatically not what the bottle “goes for.” It is one number, stated in advance, that we would hand over in a store without feeling stupid the next morning. Sometimes it lands well under MSRP. That is the point — MSRP is a suggestion from someone with an interest in the outcome.

You are allowed to pay above your number. Birthdays exist, so do bottles that mean something. Just know when you’re buying whiskey and when you’re buying a story.
